How to Say I'm Sorry • Part 3

Sorry Isn't Enough

Learning to Say “I Was Wrong — Will You Forgive Me?”

Bishop Darlingston Johnson • Harvest Intercontinental Church-Olney

Matthew 5:23-24 • James 3:17 • August 3, 2026

Let's Talk, Not Preach

“That's why I got my chair, my stool — because if I stand, I'll be tempted to preach.” This message came as a discussion, with microphones on the floor and the congregation answering back.

Another Piece of Reconciliation

The series has been about relationships and reconciliation. This session takes up the half we rarely talk about: not being forgiven, but asking to be.

The Assumption I Got Wrong

“This is something I have never, never taught about, because I always assumed everybody knew how to do it — and the only reason they didn't do it was because they didn't want to.”

“But as I was preparing and studying, I realized I made a wrong assumption. Not everybody knows how to do it.”

The Text

"Therefore if you bring your gift to the altar, and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar, and go your way. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift."

Matthew 5:23-24 (NKJV)

The Message Bible says: abandon your gift.

Read It Carefully

Not: you have something against him

That is the forgiveness half — already covered.

But: your brother has something against you

You said something or did something that wronged your brother or sister. You are the offender in this verse.

Reconciliation Has Two Halves

We've emphasized the need to forgive: don't even wait for them to acknowledge it — give them the gift of forgiveness, and give yourself the gift of freedom. That's true. But that's only one half.

The Half We Skip

“Besides my willingness to forgive you, I need to be just as willing to say to you: Will you forgive me? I was wrong.”

And be honest — for many of us, forgiving you is the easier of the two.

The Hardest Three Words

“I was wrong.”

Some of us can say “I'm sorry.” But to say “I was wrong” — for many of us that is so hard. “I guess that's why Jesus had to command it: because it doesn't happen easily.”

Part 1

Jesus Overrules Our Instinct

What we would advise — and what He actually said

Leave the Gift

What I would have told you: “Go ahead, offer your offering. Do the worship you came to do. Afterwards, go and find the person and try to fix it.”

What Jesus Actually Said

“Leave the offering. If you've got to miss the church praise and worship that Sunday — miss it. Go and say: I'm sorry, will you forgive me. And when you have taken care of that, then come back.”

“Anybody done that lately?”

A Real Answer From the Room

Asked how she would obey this command, Anita said she would call Peter:

"Brother Peter, I'm sorry for everything I did to you. Please forgive me. I'm in the temple of God, I need to give my offering. Please let this thing go — let bygones be bygones."

A sincere attempt — but is it a good apology?

“I Would Hang Up the Phone”

“Because you're not being serious.”

“You call me because you want to present your offering. We have not discussed the issue.”

“He's doing it for his own good.”

Not for the pure reason of asking forgiveness.

“It gives her leverage to give her offering.”

The bottom line: it does not seem sincere.

She may have been perfectly sincere — but she didn't know how to say “I'm sorry.”

Not a Legal Formality

Obedience here is not going through the motions, treating it legalistically, thinking words alone are enough — as if once you say “I am sorry,” the other person is obligated to accept it and everything snaps back together.

“Sometimes when someone says sorry to you, it makes you more angry.” That kind of sorry doesn't land well — it can leave the other person more upset than before.

Part 2

The Languages of Apology

Sincere — but speaking the wrong language

Like the Five Love Languages

People experience love in different ways. In marriage, what one spouse considers an expression of love may be nothing like what the other spouse needs in order to feel loved.

And the Same Is True of Apologies

The researchers behind the love languages found there are languages of apology too — and not everybody speaks the same one.

Twelve Long-Stemmed Roses

“I went out of my way — because I don't usually do these kinds of things — and I bought twelve red… my wife reminded me, long-stemmed. I don't know the difference, but she said long-stemmed. And they're not cheap, eh?”

A few days later the roses were nowhere to be seen. “What happened with the roses?” — “Oh, I forgot; it's in the trunk of the car.” By the time they went back, the roses had all withered.

The Lesson

“My wife doesn't interpret roses — even twelve of them — as an expression of love. That was the last time I had to pay money for roses.”

I Was Speaking French

“My wife understands English — and I was speaking French.
Je t'aime, ma chérie.”

“And now I can get upset with her when she says you don't show me that you love me. ‘What do you mean? I bought you twelve roses! All the bills I paid! I work hard, I come home tired — for you and your children.’” As sincere as you may be, the language you are speaking is not her language.

Your Language Is Not Their Language

“For me, honestly, all I need is ‘I'm sorry, Bishop.’ For the most part I don't require anything more.”

But here is where it goes wrong: to assume that because all I need is “I am sorry,” therefore all you should need is “I am sorry” — and if you don't accept it, you've got the problem.

“That would be wrong. Because just as with the love languages, I'm not speaking your language of apology simply by saying ‘I'm sorry.’”

Part 3

What Makes an Apology Real

Answers from the congregation

Name the Thing

“First, it is important to name the thing — to name the grievance, and to accept that you have wronged the person.”

The Common Dodge

“I did not know you would feel that way.” “Sorry you felt that way.” — It is not about how the person felt. It is about the act of grievance you committed.

“I'm Sorry You Feel That Way”

Say that, and you are not admitting you were wrong. In fact you may be saying what I did was right, and I would do it again — I'm just sorry about your reaction.

“Really, I'm blaming you for your feelings. I didn't do anything that I think should have made you feel that way — but since you feel that way, okay, I'm sorry for how you feel, not for what I did.”

“You're actually assigning blame — not intentionally, but you're not admitting you're wrong.”

Be Specific: “I Was Wrong For…”

For an apology to be genuine and full, be very specific about what you did or said that was wrong: “I said this. I did that. And I was wrong for saying it. And that has caused you to feel the way you do. I'm sorry for what I did.”

Why the Apology Isn't Landing

If someone isn't receiving your apology, maybe it's because you only said “I'm sorry,” or only “I'm sorry for how you feel” — you never fully admitted that you were wrong.

Show Me Change

“One of the things I would expect to see is a change in behavior. If the apology was genuine, the patterns that existed before it would have changed.”

Words are a good start. But not “I was sorry, I wronged you” — and then I am fully prepared to do it again.

Say It Out Loud

“I want to let you know that, with God's help, I will never do that again.” Then let the actions follow: “I know it's juicy right now — but no, I'm a changed person; I'm being changed by the grace of God.”

Tone and Remorse

“Before looking for the changes, I look for the person being remorseful. I look at the tone of the apology. Those two things really matter for me.”

Verbal and Nonverbal

How you say something is as important as what you say — sometimes more important. How you express your regret is what makes it believable.

Don't Manufacture an Apology

A young voice pushed back: “If I tell you something and I genuinely mean it, why would I apologize for something that I mean? Then I'm only apologizing because that's what you want to hear.”

The answer: if you haven't done wrong — if the accusation isn't true — don't go and apologize for it. “You didn't do it. He didn't say go and make up something to say ‘I'm sorry’ for.”

But if you did say it or do it — even if you never intended harm, and harm was caused — then Jesus says: be humble enough to say “I was wrong.”

The 90/10 Principle

When two people clash, nobody comes out perfect. Maybe she was ninety percent at fault in the way she spoke to you. That still doesn't justify how you spoke back.

“Jesus didn't say if the fault is all yours. He didn't say if the person didn't contribute to the cause. The fact is — you could have responded differently.”

Own Your Ten Percent

“Listen — when you spoke to me that way, I became angry, and I said things I should not have said. I'm sorry for responding the way I did.”

Say It With Me

“One wrong doesn't justify another wrong.”

“Don't give yourself a free pass just because you didn't cause it, you didn't initiate it. You are not fully to blame — but don't give yourself a free pass for acting wrongly in response.”

Part 4

Receiving an Apology

This works both ways

Don't Make It Hard

“Some of us don't know how to release someone: you did this to me, and I'm going to keep holding it against you — no matter how many times you say I'm sorry, I'm still insisting you haven't proven it yet.”

If Jesus tells the offender to drop everything and come, then you to whom he comes must also be gracious. “But somehow we sometimes want to punish the person — and we're making it hard.”

“I've got to work on my ability and willingness to receive apologies.”

Quick to Listen

"Let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath."

James 1:19 (NKJV)

When someone comes with an apology, the meek posture is to be eager to listen — wanting to resolve the issue, not to prove you're right. “You're listening for understanding, not to find fault. Listen beyond the words — listen to the person's heart, even if they are not using the right words.”

Wisdom Manifests Meekness

"Who is wise and understanding among you? Let him show by good conduct that his works are done in the meekness of wisdom."

James 3:13 (NKJV)

“Anytime you're not operating in meekness, you're being foolish.” You may still need to give correction — but give it with meekness and humility. Don't add insult. They came to apologize; don't insult them some more.

Easy to Entreat

"But the wisdom that is from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, willing to yield, full of mercy and good fruits, without partiality and without hypocrisy."

James 3:17 (NKJV)

“Willing to yield” — other translations say easy to entreat. “Please don't make it hard. Make it easy for someone to apologize and say I'm sorry.”

Boundaries and Rebuilt Trust

“I'm not saying you immediately become best friends, because trust is something that has to be built over time. If the repentance was real, time will show it.”

Both at Once

“Maybe there are certain boundaries I still have — but I'm accepting your apology, because I am easy to be entreated. I am not difficult and hard. I'm humble, I'm gentle. I will treat you the same way I want to be treated.”

Copy the Savior

From the floor: “If indeed we understand that we have Christ in us, we'll be the people to really be easy to forgive. We will be the people not to hold grudges — because we are trying to be the light, we are living to be salt.”

“When somebody slaps you on this cheek, turn the other. Why? Because you have the characteristic of the Savior. You are copying the Savior — not the tradition, not your tribe where you came from.”

“We have left the kingdom of darkness for the kingdom of Jesus Christ. If you don't take up grudges, you are doing yourself a favor — because that's how Christ will be seen in us.”

Part 5

Why Jesus Said It

Because relationships are worship

Worship Is More Than the Altar

Love God with all your heart and mind — and love your neighbour as yourself. As important as raising hands, singing songs, and giving gifts is, Jesus is saying worship is more than that.

Your relationship with people — how you treat them, how you talk to them — is also worship. “And in fact, that kind of worship is even greater than the worship we do when we come to church.”

He never said don't come to the altar, don't bring your offering, don't come to church. He said: if you are serious about worshiping in spirit and in truth, you will not minimize your relationships with people.

The Highest Form of Worship

“When you're saying I'm sorry — that's real worship.
When you're saying will you forgive me? —
man, that's worship at the next level.”

Each person is made in the image of God. How you treat a person made in the image of God is how you treat God. “If you can't love the person you can see, how will you love the God you cannot see?”

Part 6

The Five R's

The elements of a complete, full apology

“We need to become experts at saying: I'm sorry, I was wrong.”

1. Express Regret

Genuine regret — not “I'm sorry if I hurt you.” That's not genuine.

What It Sounds Like

“I'm sorry that I did this. I said this, and I am genuinely sorry. I could have handled this differently. And because I did that, it cost you this.”

Express real sorrow. Most people want a little bit more than just the words “I'm sorry.”

2. Accept Responsibility

“I was wrong, but you shouldn't have done this.” “I was wrong, but the way I was feeling that day, you should not have provoked me.”

"Once you put 'but,' it means you're about to raise a defense — and usually 'but' is strong enough to cancel everything that comes before it. So when you say 'I'm sorry, but…' you just cancelled everything you said."

Bishop Darlingston Johnson

Don't justify your actions. Name what you said and did that was wrong. Make no excuse.

3. Offer Restitution

“Most of the time we don't get to do this — or we don't offer to. But in the biblical narrative, the third element of a full apology is to offer restitution when possible.”

Ask: “Is there anything I can do? How can I make this better, fix what I did, give back what I took from you?” Many will say “don't worry about it” — but the offer itself proves the ownership.

It's Not Only About Money

If you embarrassed someone in front of people, restitution means going back to those same people and saying: “I said this, and I was wrong. That lady didn't deserve it.” You are restoring the dignity you took.

4. Commit to Repentance

Commit to change: “This behaviour was wrong. The way I spoke was wrong. And God being my helper, I am going to change this type of behaviour. I will not do this again, in Jesus' name.”

"Bear fruits worthy of repentance."

Matthew 3:8 — John the Baptist (NKJV)

5. Request Forgiveness

“Learn to say: I did wrong. I take responsibility for it. But don't assume that just because you confess, the person is forced to forgive you.”

Go ahead and ask: “Will you forgive me?” And then it is up to that person to say yes — or not.

“If they refuse, and you know you have done everything you know to do — leave it with them and God. But make sure you have gone through a true and sincere expression of your sorrow for what you said or did.”

Go and Do It

“This is not just a message to give you knowledge or information. This is something you're supposed to act upon.”

If You Are the Offender

“Listen — I said this, I did this. I was angry, but I was wrong. And I want to say I'm sorry. Will you forgive me?” Go ahead and do it. You're doing the will of God.

If You Are the Offended

“Go ahead now and be gracious. Don't demand perfection. If you know they're trying, extend the forgiveness and make room for reconciliation — and where you need to explain something, do it with meekness. Be merciful. Be gentle. Be peaceable.”

Study Notes

Sermon Summary

Sorry Isn't Enough — Bishop Darlingston Johnson

Key points, principles, and takeaways

Overview

Part 3 of the reconciliation series, delivered deliberately as a seated discussion rather than a sermon, with the congregation answering from the floor. Bishop Johnson opens with a confession: he had never taught this subject because he assumed everyone already knew how to apologize and simply chose not to. Studying it changed his mind — not everybody knows how.

From Matthew 5:23-24, Jesus commands the offender to abandon his gift at the altar and go be reconciled first. That is the half of reconciliation we skip: we teach forgiving, but not asking to be forgiven. And because people have apology languages just as they have love languages, sincerity alone is not enough — a sincere apology in the wrong language lands like twelve long-stemmed roses left withering in the trunk of a car.

The message closes with the five R's of a complete apology — Regret, Responsibility, Restitution, Repentance, and a Request for forgiveness — framed by the sermon's largest claim: how you treat people is worship, and it may be worship greater than what happens at the altar.

Summary 1 of 7

Key Scriptures

Matthew 5:23-24 — "If you bring your gift to the altar, and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there… first be reconciled to your brother, and then come." The anchor text. Note the direction: your brother has something against you — you are the offender. The Message renders "leave" as abandon.

James 1:19 — "Swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath." Applied here not to receiving the Word but to receiving an apology: listen for understanding, not to find fault; listen to the heart even when the words are wrong.

James 3:13 — "Who is wise and understanding among you? Let him show by good conduct that his works are done in the meekness of wisdom." Wisdom always manifests meekness; therefore any unmeek response to an apology is, by definition, foolish.

James 3:17-18 — the wisdom from above is "pure, peaceable, gentle, willing to yield, full of mercy and good fruits." "Willing to yield" is elsewhere rendered easy to entreat — the sermon's governing phrase for how to receive an apology.

Matthew 3:8 — John the Baptist: "Bear fruits worthy of repentance." Cited to establish that repentance in an apology must be visible in changed behaviour.

1 John 4:20 — "If you can't love the person you can see, how will you love the God you cannot see?" The bridge from relationships to worship.

Matthew 5:39 / the Beatitudes — raised from the floor: turning the other cheek is copying the Savior, not the tradition or the tribe.

Summary 2 of 7

Main Teachings (I)

Reconciliation has two halves — the church has been taught to forgive without waiting for acknowledgement. But the other half is being just as willing to say "Will you forgive me? I was wrong." For most of us, forgiving is the easier of the two.

Jesus overrules our sensible instinct — the Bishop's own advice would have been "worship first, fix it afterwards." Jesus said the opposite: leave the offering, miss the praise and worship if you must, go and reconcile, and then come back. He commanded it precisely because it doesn't happen easily.

Sincerity is not the same as competence — Anita's proposed phone call ("I'm sorry for everything I did to you; I'm at the temple and need to give my offering") was genuinely meant, yet three congregants said they'd hang up. She was sincere; she simply didn't know how to say it.

An apology can make things worse — a badly delivered "sorry" often leaves the offended party angrier than before, because it reads as clearing a legal requirement rather than seeking a person.

People have apology languages — the researchers behind the five love languages found the same variation in apologies. What fully satisfies you may communicate nothing to the person you wronged.

Summary 3 of 7

Main Teachings (II)

The roses in the trunk — twelve long-stemmed roses, bought at real cost by a man who "doesn't usually do these kinds of things," ended up forgotten in the car boot until they withered. The gift was expensive and sincere and still communicated nothing, because his wife does not read roses as love. "I was speaking French; my wife speaks English."

Name the grievance — a real apology identifies the specific act: "I said this, I did that, and I was wrong for it." It is not about how the person felt; it is about the act you committed.

"I'm sorry you feel that way" is blame in disguise — it admits nothing, may even imply the act was right and would be repeated, and quietly assigns responsibility for the injury to the injured party's feelings.

Repentance belongs inside the apology — words are a good start, but the congregation wanted changed patterns of behaviour, and the commitment stated aloud: "with God's help, I will never do that again."

Tone is part of the message — remorse and delivery matter; how you say it is as important as what you say, and sometimes more so.

Don't manufacture an apology — if you genuinely did not do the thing you're accused of, don't confess to it. Jesus never asked anyone to invent a wrong. But if you did cause harm, even unintentionally, be humble enough to say "I was wrong."

Summary 4 of 7

The Five R's of a Full Apology

1. Regret — genuine sorrow, not "I'm sorry if I hurt you." Name the cost: "I could have handled this differently, and because I did that, it cost you this." Most people want more than the bare words.

2. Responsibility — accept it without justification, and above all without "but." "Once you put 'but,' you're about to raise a defense… and 'but' is strong enough to cancel everything that comes before it." Make no excuse.

3. Restitution — the element most often skipped. Ask "Is there anything I can do? How can I give back what I took?" Illustrated with money (don't go silent on a debt you can't repay — explain your plan), but applied more broadly: if you embarrassed someone publicly, go back to those same people and restore the dignity you took.

4. Repentance / Reformation — commit to change: "God being my helper, I am going to change this behaviour. I will not do this again, in Jesus' name." Bear fruits worthy of repentance (Matthew 3:8).

5. Request forgiveness — actually ask: "Will you forgive me?" Confession does not obligate the other person to forgive. If they refuse and you have done everything you know to do, leave it with them and God.

Summary 5 of 7

Principles & Warnings

One wrong doesn't justify another wrong. The 90/10 principle: even if the other party is ninety percent at fault, you are still responsible for your ten percent. Jesus never made apologizing conditional on being the sole cause.

Relationships are worship — and treating one another rightly may be worship greater than what happens at the altar. Each person bears God's image, so how you treat them is how you treat God.

Wisdom always manifests meekness — therefore an unmeek response is a foolish one, however correct its content.

Trust rebuilds on a different timeline than forgiveness — accepting an apology is immediate; becoming best friends again is not. Boundaries may remain while forgiveness is fully extended.

Warning — don't give yourself a free pass. "You didn't initiate it, you're not fully to blame" is not permission to keep your part of the wrong.

Warning — don't weaponize the verse. Someone asked whether "leave your gift" becomes an excuse to skip church or withhold giving. The Bishop's answer: anyone using it that way has a deeper problem than the apology; that is making it worse, not better.

Warning — don't punish the person who came. Some make apology so hard that no one can ever reach "I received it." Don't add insult to someone who came to make peace, and don't demand perfection from an imperfect apology.

Warning — don't assume your standard is universal. "All I need is 'I'm sorry' — therefore all you should need is 'I'm sorry'" is exactly the roses-in-the-trunk mistake.

Summary 6 of 7

Takeaways & Declarations

Identify the person — "there are some people you need to go to, and you know that you haven't gone to them and really said 'I was wrong.'" Name them today.

Drop the "but" — audit your next apology for the word that cancels it.

Be specific — replace "I'm sorry if you were hurt" with "I said this, and I was wrong for saying it."

Offer restitution — ask what you can do to repair it, even when you expect the answer to be "don't worry about it."

Learn their language — find out what the person you wronged actually needs to hear, rather than what satisfies you.

Make yourself easy to reach — if someone has been trying to apologize, tell them today that you have received it.

Act, don't just know — "This is not just a message to give you knowledge or information. This is something you're supposed to act upon."

Declarations — "I am sorry. I was wrong." "One wrong doesn't justify another wrong." "'But' cancels everything that comes before it." "Make it easy for someone to apologize." "When you're saying 'I'm sorry,' that's real worship — when you're saying 'will you forgive me?', that's worship at the next level." And the closing charge: be merciful, be gentle, and be peaceable.

But remember: one of the most important ways of worship is the way we treat one another. Each person is made in the image of God. And so how you treat a person who's made in the image of God is how you treat God.

The Five R's. So quickly, let me give you five R's — a complete, full apology that has all the elements in it. And all of us should try to grow and perfect this. We need to become experts at saying "I'm sorry, I was wrong." One: express regret. Genuine regret. Not "I'm sorry if I hurt you" — that's not genuine. "I'm sorry that I did this. I said this, and I'm genuinely sorry. I could have handled this differently. And because I did this or that, it cost you this." Express real sorrow. Often it's more than simply "I'm sorry." Most people want a little bit more than that.

Secondly: take or accept responsibility for what you did. Don't say, "I was wrong, but you shouldn't have done this." "I was wrong, but the way I was feeling that day, you should not have provoked me." Whatever it is — once you put "but," it means you're about to raise a defense. You're setting up your argument against. You're about to prove why you're not as bad as she thinks. And usually "but" is strong enough to cancel everything that comes before it. So when you say "I'm sorry, but…" you just cancelled everything you said before. So accepting responsibility means you don't try to justify your actions. Determine what you said that was wrong, what you did that was wrong, and accept responsibility. Make no excuse.

Third: offer restitution when possible. Most of the time we don't get to do this, or we don't offer to. But when you look at the biblical narrative, the third thing that should be part of a full apology is to offer restitution when possible. If because of what you did you took something from someone — yeah, I say I'm sorry, take responsibility, but then ask them: "Is there anything I can do? How can I make this better, fix what I did? How can I give you back what I took from you, if it's possible?" Most people will appreciate the fact that you even want to make restitution. They may say, "Don't worry about it, that's fine." But the fact that you take responsibility enough that you want to make restitution… I'm going to use money, because it's just easy to illustrate. If I lend you money and you're not able to repay, you need to tell me. Don't just say, "Oh, I'm sorry," and then not tell me how you plan to — okay, you weren't able to do it, but this is what I'm working on, or this is why. In other words, unless the person releases you from the responsibility to give back what was given to you, you have to carry a sense of responsibility: it is my responsibility to make restitution. Talk to the person. Many people say, "That's okay, that's fine, I understand, you didn't mean it." But don't feel that you don't have a responsibility to do everything within your power to make right what you did that was wrong. I use money just because it's easy, but it's anything. If you embarrass somebody because of the way you talked to them before people — restitution would mean that you've got to call the people before whom you did the embarrassment and say to them, "Listen, I said this, I was wrong. That lady didn't deserve it." You follow me? So you're trying to restore her dignity, because you took something from her by the way you talked.

Fourth: commit to repentance or reformation. Commit to change. "Okay, this behavior was wrong. The way I spoke was wrong. And God being my helper, I am going to change this type of behavior. I will not do this again, in Jesus' name." John the Baptist said you should bring forth fruits worthy of repentance. Hallelujah.

And then lastly: request forgiveness. Learn to say, "I did wrong. I take responsibility for it." But don't assume that just because you confess, the person is forced to forgive you. You go ahead and request it: "Will you forgive me?" or "Please forgive me." And then it's up to that person to say yes, I forgive you — or not. If they refuse to forgive you, and you know you have done everything you know to do to express it, then you've got to leave it with her and God, or with him and God. But just make sure you have gone through a true and sincere expression of your sorrow as a result of what you said or did.

Go and Do It. So then, if we're willing to offer forgiveness, and we know how to receive forgiveness, then God's will can be done, and we can start operating in peace and in harmony. This is not just a message to give you knowledge or information. This is something you're supposed to act upon. There may be someone, or people, who you know you said things to or did things to, and you have maybe justified it as a reaction to some wrong that they did. The wrong they did doesn't justify any wrong you did. If you need to go back and say, "Listen, I said this, I did this, I was angry, but I was wrong. And I want to say I'm sorry. Will you forgive me?" — go ahead and do it. You're doing the will of God. And if somebody has said "I'm sorry" but you have refused to accept it — go ahead now and be gracious. Don't demand perfection. If you know that they're trying, go ahead and extend the forgiveness, make room for possible reconciliation. And where you need to talk to them to explain certain things, do it with the spirit of meekness and gentleness. But be merciful. Be gentle. And be peaceable. In Jesus' name.
